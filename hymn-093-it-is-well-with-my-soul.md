# It Is Well with My Soul


**Date/source note:** 1873

## Background

1873 Many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the Lord delivers them out of them
all. Psalm 34 verse 19 When the great Chicago fire consumed the windy city in 1871,
Horatio G. Spafford, an attorney heavily invested in real estate, lost a fortune. One at
that time his only son, age four, succumbed to Scarlet Fever. Horatio drowned his grief
and work, pouring himself into rebuilding the city, and assisting the one hundred
thousand who had been left homeless. In November of 1873 he decided to take his wife and
daughters to Europe.

Horatio was close to D.L. Moody and Iris Sanky, and he wanted to visit their
evangelistic meetings in England, then enjoy a vacation. In an urgent matter detained
Horatio in New York, he decided to send his wife Anna and their four daughters Maggie,
Tannetta, Annie, and Bessie on ahead. As he saw them settled into a cabin aboard the
luxurious French liner, Ville du Havera, an unease filled his mind, and he moved them to
a room closer to the bow of the ship, then he said goodbye promising to join them soon.
In the small hours of November 22, 1873, as the Ville du Havera glided over smooth seas,
the passengers were jolted from their bunks.

The ship had collided with an iron sailing vessel, and water poured in like Niagara. The
Ville du Havera tilted dangerously. Screams, prayers, and oaths merged into a nightmare
of unmeasured terror. The ship's clung to posts, tumbled through darkness, and were
swept away by powerful currents of icy ocean. Loved ones fell from each other's grasp
and disappeared into foaming blackness. Within two hours the mighty ship vanished
beneath the waters. The 226 fatalities included Maggie, Tannetta, Annie, and Bessie.
Mrs. Spafford was found nearly unconscious, clinging to a piece of the wreckage.

From the forty-seven survivors landed in cart of whales, she cabled her husband, saved,
alone. Her ratio immediately booked passage to join his wife. In route on a cold
December night the captain called him aside and said, I believe we are now passing over
the place where the Ville du Havera went down. Spafford went to his cabin but found it
hard to sleep. He said to himself, it is well. The will of God be done. He later wrote
his famous hymn based on those words. The melody for it is well titled Ville du Havera
was written by Philip Bliss, who was himself soon to perish along with his wife in a
terrible train wreck in Ohio
