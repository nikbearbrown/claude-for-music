**Date/source note:** 1873

## Background

Francis Ridley Havardgall and Francis Fanny Crosby never met, but they became dear pin-
pals, the two most famous women himnest of their age, the former in England and the

Are the notes of one who may never see, visible music of flower and tree? Oh, her heart
can see, her heart can see, and its sight is strong and swift and free. Another of
Fanny's dearest friends was Phoebe Napp. While Fanny lived in the Manhattan slums and
worked in rescue missions, Phoebe lived in the Napp mansion, a palatial residence in
Brooklyn, where she entertained lavishly. She was an extravagant dresser with a wardrobe
full of elaborate gowns and diamond tiaras. Her music room contained one of the finest
collections of instruments in the country, and Fanny was a frequent housecast.

One day in 1873, while Fanny was staying at the Napp mansion, Phoebe said she had a tune
she wanted to play. Going to the music room, she set at the piano and played a new
composition of her own, while the blind himnest listened. Fanny immediately clapped her
hands and exclaimed, why that says, bless at assurance. She quickly composed the words
and a great hymn was born. Many years later, D.L. Moody was preaching in New York at the
23rd Street Dutch Reformed Church. The Moody Sankie meetings had popularized Fanny
Crosby's hymns around the world and had made the blind poetess a household name.

But whenever she attended a Moody Sankie meeting, she refused to be recognized,
disavowing a claim. This day the church was so crowded she could find nowhere to set.
Moody's son Will, seeing her, offered to find her a seat. To her bewilderment, he let
her on to the platform just as the crowd was singing, blessed assurance. Moody Sr.
jumped to his feet, raised his hand and interrupted the singing. Praise the Lord, he
shouted. Here comes the authoress. Fanny took her seat amid thunderous ovation, humbly
thanking God for making her a blessing to so many
