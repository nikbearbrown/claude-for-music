# O Master, Let Me Walk with Thee


**Date/source note:** 1879

## Background

1879. Therefore, you shall keep the commandments of the Lord your God, to walk in His
ways and to fear Him. Deuteronomy, chapter 8, verse 6. Washington, Gladden, so named
because his great-grandfather served his George Washington's bodyguard during the
Revolutionary War, was a congregational pastor. He served in New York, Massachusetts,
and Ohio during the post-civil war industrial era when racial and economic injustice was
rampant. People everywhere were searching for a new American ethic. Washington wanted to
give them one, and he is remembered today as the father of the social gospel in America,
an activist who crusaded tirelessly for a political and moral reform in industry,
commerce, and politics.

He was a champion of the working man, a strong supporter of Union rights, and he was
unafraid to attack corruption and politics. Unfortunately, Washington advanced the
liberal-leaning biblical criticism that undercut conservative theology at the turn of
the century. It was more concerned with applied Christianity than with biblical
Christianity. He wrote 38 books on such themes. In one of them he said, the Bible is not
an infallible book in the sense in which it is popularly supposed to be infallible. The
book is not infallible historically.

It is not infallible scientifically. It is not infallible morally. He embraced evolution
and opposed the outreach efforts of evangelists like Billy Sunday. He infuriated his own
denomination by railing against a large gift to foreign missions because it was donated
by the standard oil company. Throughout surprisingly, conservative Christians opposed
Washington's liberal theology. During a period of heavy criticism he sat alone in his
church and wrote this hymn. Dr. Charles H. Richards later saw it in a magazine, and
while loving the poem was troubled by the second verse
