# Jesus, Lover of My Soul


**Date/source note:** 1740

## Background

1740. For He Himself has said, I will never leave you nor forsake you, so we may boldly
say, the Lord is my helper, I will not fear. What can man do to me? Hebrews chapter 13
verses 5b through 6. Many stories have arisen around the writing of Jesus, lover of my
soul, but they all appear to be fictional. We don't know the exact occasion for his
composition, but it was written shortly after Charles Wesley's conversion, and its words
seem to anticipate the huge crowds, lawless mobs, midnight escapes, traveling dangers
and flea-infested beds, he will encounter incoming years.

Wesley's life and brief can be summed up as follows. As the Methodist movement spread
through England, Charles traveled horseback from place to place, an itinerate homeless
evangelist, his fiery preaching incited revival in some people, and outrage in others.
In the midst of all this, his dear mother Susanna died on July 23, 1742. Her last words
were, children, as soon as I am released, sing a song of praise to God. Later preaching
in Wales, Charles met Sally Gwynn, a beautiful girl half his age, a courtship followed,
and Charles wanted to propose, but he was virtually penniless with no way of supporting
a wife.

That's when he decided to publish his hymns and sacred poems, as well as his journals
and sermons, hoping the royalties would provide an income. Charles and Sally were
married on April 8, 1749. Charles noting, not a cloud was to be seen from morning till
night. I rose at four, spent three hours in the half in prayer, or singing with my
brother. At eight I led my Sally to church. It was the most solemn season of love. They
left immediately on a preaching tour, and Charles continued his itinerate ministry until
1756, when at age 49 exhausted, he and Sally settled down.

Charles visited himself, preaching, visiting, counseling, fretting about his three
unsaved children, trying to keep methodism within the Church of England, and giving
unsolicited advice to his brother John. All the while he worked tirelessly on his hymns
and poems. By early 1788 Charles was bedfast, not from sickness, but from a lifetime of
fatigue. By March two week to right he dictated his last hymn to Sally. In age and
feebleness extreme, who shall essential worm redeem? Jesus my only hope thou art,
strength of my failing flesh and heart.

O could I catch a smile from thee and drop into eternity?
